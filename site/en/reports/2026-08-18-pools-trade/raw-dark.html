0. Origin: the SOL nobody can take out

While designing an IDO fundraising module, I arrived at an uncomfortable conclusion.

The rules are simple: 100 SOL cap per sale, 5 SOL per address; the money raised buys straight on the bonding curve, and then everyone receives tokens at the latest price after the buying is done. That way everyone's cost equals the opening price and nobody can arbitrage the spread — if you distributed at the average price, selling immediately would be risk-free profit.

The cost is a leftover chunk of tokens that never lands in anyone's pocket. So when everyone sells together, the SOL they get back is always less than the 100 SOL they put in, and the difference stays in the pool forever. The platform didn't take it, users didn't, and the project can't either.

MeWho does that unrecoverable SOL serve?

DevIt props up pool depth, so the chart is harder to dump through.

MeSo the beneficiary is the chart, not people. And on the day it goes to zero it gets swapped out — by the last sellers, not by the people who put the money in.

DevBy that logic, the long-term beneficiaries are the chain, and whoever runs fastest.

A crowd of retail buyers pooled money to make a permanent donation to a chain's pool, and subsidized the fastest runners along the way.

I wanted to know how others handle this, and found pools.trade — Uniswap itself, on a new chain, with liquidity explicitly permanently locked. The first answer is in §2.9: it isn't a design flaw, it's a curve you can draw precisely. The second answer is in §7.4.

How to read this: chapters 1–6 are mechanism and on-chain data, written as far as possible only from what transaction hashes can prove; chapter 7 is my four judgments as a product manager. If you don't want opinions, skip straight to chapter 8.

1. Demand: what is Uniswap actually solving

1.1 The stated problem: three structural flaws of bonding curves

The CCA whitepaper (whitepaper_cca.pdf, 2025-11, authors including Hayden Adams and Ciamac Moallemi) states the goal plainly, in just two points: price discovery — "determining a fair, credible market price under uncertainty and information asymmetry"; liquidity bootstrapping — "ensuring enough capital forms to support active, stable trading while price stays tied to the latest information".

Its critique of existing designs is where the demand comes from. On pump.fun-style bonding curves: "Its core fragility is one-shot execution: a buyer's transaction executes immediately against the current AMM state, making the outcome highly dependent on transaction timing, mempool ordering and execution latency, and thus exposed to MEV attacks such as front-running and sandwiching." On traditional uniform-price / Dutch auctions: "Because the clearing price depends on total demand, bidders have no incentive to bid early, so participation concentrates at the last moment and turns into sniping on-chain."

Put the two critiques together and you get CCA's design constraints: (a) no one-shot fills, or it becomes a race of reflexes; (b) no settlement only at the end, or it becomes a race for the last second. The answer is "continuous clearing" — slice supply into tens of thousands of blocks released step by step, fill a small slice at a uniform price in each block, and give money that arrives early a price advantage.

1.2 The unstated demand: cold-starting a new chain and owning the issuance entry point

Reading only the whitepaper underestimates this product. Robinhood Chain is an Arbitrum Orbit chain launched in 2026 (chainId 4663, ~100ms blocks). For Uniswap, pools.trade solves several more commercial problems at once:

2.2 Instant Launch: every parameter is fixed in the contracts

Two strategy contracts correspond to "creator fee on" and "creator fee off", both with verified source: 0x23f82095… (fee ON, with FeeSplitter 0xeFF166AA…) and 0xad44D55E… (fee OFF). Hard-coded constants: TOTAL_SUPPLY = 1e9e18, LP_FEE = 2500 (0.25%), TICK_SPACING = 25, MIN_LAUNCH_TICK = −160,100, MAX_INITIAL_TICK = 251,325.

The creator can buy in the same transaction (distributeWithNative), which is the anti-sniping design the team describes as "the creator buys in the same block the token launches" — in substance it reserves the first shot for the creator rather than eliminating sniping. Example: NODEBOX's creation transaction 0x68a09148… creates the token and self-buys 0.1 ETH in one multicall.

2.3 Crowd Launch timing: 4 hours split into 13 steps

This is the most misunderstood part. The window is 144,000 blocks ≈ 4 hours (100ms blocks). The supply release schedule is stored on-chain as SSTORE2 data (Robincat's pointer 0x6123b3a5…); decoded, it is 13 pairs of (uint24 mps, uint40 blockDelta), and the mps sum to exactly 1e7 (100%).

Each of the first 12 steps releases ~5.8%, and the number of blocks per step decreases (18,157 → 10,072), meaning supply is released faster and faster per unit of time. The last block then releases the remaining 30.01% (about 150M tokens) in one go.

This "30% all at the end" design has a clear purpose: it guarantees there is still plenty of supply to fill at the final moment, so any order submitted within the window still has a chance to be filled; and it stops the final clearing price from being inflated by "nothing left to sell". The side effect: the last block carries 30% of the whole sale's volume, and whoever's order is still alive at that moment (maxPrice above the clearing price) takes that chunk.

2.4 How the clearing price forms: three invariants

The core logic is in ContinuousClearingAuction.sol's _checkpointAtBlock() and _iterateOverTicksAndFindClearingPrice(). It boils down to three invariants:

  1. Per-block uniform price: every buyer filled in the same block pays exactly the same price; there is no ordering advantage. Order within a block has no effect on the fill price.
  2. Only goes up (ratchet): the source comment states "The new clearing price can never be lower than the current clearing price". The clearing price is a monotonically non-decreasing ratchet. Cancelling orders doesn't pull it back.
  3. Rounded up to the tick: the price can only land on integer multiples of tickSpacing; Robincat's tickSpacing = 1% of the floor price, so the price ladder moves in 1% steps.

Per-block pricing formula (verified with real data):

QuantityFormula
Remaining-progress factor ff = (1e7 − cumulativeMps) / 1e7
Clearing price PP = max( P_prev , ceil_tick( D_eff × f / S_remaining ) )
Raised in this blockraised_block = D_eff × deltaMps / 1e7
Tokens filled in this blocktokens_block = raised_block / P; unsold supply rolls over to the next block

The price changes block by block, not step by step. Every block creates a checkpoint and recomputes the clearing price; the 13-step table only decides "how much supply this block releases" — each step's mps is a per-block release rate (32 mps per block in step 1, 58 in step 12, 3,001,126 in the last block). So a 4-hour crowd sale logically has 144,000 clearing prices; step changes only alter the release speed, not the pricing frequency.

Here D_eff is the sum of the "effective amounts" of all orders whose maxPrice is strictly above the current clearing price. The contract keeps history in an inverse-price accumulator cumulativeMpsPerPrice += (deltaMps << 192) / clearingPrice, so any order can compute its tokens in O(1) when it exits — the key to keeping gas constant across 4 hours and 144,000 blocks.

2.5 Budget normalization: why orders are "always fully spent"

The order hint in the front end says "Your budget for this launch window, always fully filled." That's accurate at the contract level; the mechanism hides in an easy-to-miss normalization step.

The key is BidLib.toEffectiveAmount():

effectiveAmount = amountQ96 × 1e7 / (1e7 − bid.startCumulativeMps)

When an order enters the demand pool, its amount is first "normalized by the issuance progress remaining at submission". Submit 1 ETH at 50% progress and the effective amount entering the pool is 2 ETH. So per block the order actually spends:

spend_per_block = amount × deltaMps / (1e7 − startCumulativeMps)

Summing deltaMps over all remaining blocks gives exactly 1e7 − startCumulativeMps, so cumulative spend equals the full budget. The source comment itself explains the purpose of this step: "an important normalization step to ensure that we can calculate the currencyRaised … using the original supply schedule".

A budget goes unspent in only two cases:

CaseConsequenceMeasured
Outbid: the clearing price rises to ≥ your maxPriceThe order stops filling immediately; the unspent part is refundableSimulation: a bot with maxPrice = 20 ETH FDV, in an auction that finally ran to 340× the floor, spent only 1.8% of its budget; 98.2% was refunded
Sold out early: all 500M tokens soldThe contract rejects new orders (AuctionSoldOut); existing orders stop fillingSnowOn sold all 500M tokens; remainingSupply = 0

So the real advantage of entering early is not "spending more" but "everything you buy is cheap supply" — your budget is spread over the blocks where the clearing price was still low. The numbers are in §4.5.

2.6 maxPrice: users can't set it, but it decides everything

At the contract level submitBid(maxPriceQ96, amount, …)'s maxPrice is completely free (capped at MAX_BID_PRICE = 2.0859e39, i.e. an upper bound equivalent to ~25,000 ETH FDV); but the pools.trade front end never exposes this input, only mentioning it in a hint: "Your order stays active up to a 25,000 ETH fully diluted valuation." In other words, an ordinary user placing an order implicitly accepts "buy at any price".

This default cap is so high it can practically never be hit. A fully diluted valuation of 25,000 ETH at $1,900 per ETH is about $47.5M; yet since pools.trade launched, the highest clearing price ever reached was 493.87 ETH FDV (Absolute Goat, ~$938K, see §4.2), a 50× gap. In practice the default order is a no-limit order: it never stops filling because "the price got too high"; it just keeps buying until the budget runs out or supply sells out. That explains why in SnowOn's 306 bids, 225 had their price cap at the same value.

Three maxPrice clusters are clearly visible on-chain (measured on INCEL and Robincat):

ClustermaxPriceLikely source
Front-end default25,000 ETH FDV (≈47,000× floor)Ordinary users; of SnowOn's 306 bids, 225 fall in this cluster
Script A~20 ETH FDV (38× floor)The same address 0xDCf60e2B… repeatedly uses four fixed amounts — 0.000010 / 0.114286 / 0.057143 / 0.028571 ETH — and shows up in Robincat, HC and INCEL
Script BA few dozen ticks above the floorOnly wants floor-price supply; outbid as soon as the price moves

The product problem: maxPrice is the only risk-control knob in this mechanism, and the front end hides it. Retail can't express "the most valuation I'll accept", while scripts can — which effectively hands scripts an asymmetric tool (at the cost of possibly getting nothing).

2.7 Graduation, migration, refunds, zombie tokens

  1. Threshold check: currencyRaised ≥ requiredCurrencyRaised. Measured, the threshold is always 5× the floor FDV (Robincat 2.640532 ETH, HC 2.657584 ETH, SnowOn 2.652057 ETH), matching the "$10K FDV graduation line" shown in the front end.
  2. Migration: once the threshold is met, LBPStrategy (0x05d55239…) injects the raised ETH and the 500M reserved tokens into a Uniswap v4 pool (fee 2500, tickSpacing 50, no hook); the recipient of the LP position is FeeSplitter, not the creator — this is how "permanently locked liquidity" is implemented.
  3. Failure means refund: at the contract level it is pull-based (users call exitBid themselves); the front-end copy says "automatically refunded", a slight overstatement. In practice all 13 Robincat bids were refunded and the auction contract's ETH balance is 0; 5 of them were refunded by the creator sending an aggregate3 batch on the buyers' behalf. Funds are safe, but the experience still needs the user to sign a transaction.
  4. Zombie tokens: tokens from failed sales are already minted before the auction starts, just with no pool and zero circulation. Burning the 500M reserve requires someone to actively call migrate(), hit the catch branch and burn to 0xdEaD. Across 2,985 crowd sales since launch this has happened only twice MigrationFailed — meaning almost no failed sale is ever cleaned up, leaving thousands of permanently dangling empty tokens on-chain.

2.8 Fees in full: three numbers and one difference in basis

This is where users get confused most easily. The on-chain facts:

(1) The real total fee is 0.2899%, not 0.25%. Every pools.trade v4 pool has in slot0 lpFee = 2500, protocolFee = 1638800. 1638800 = (400 &lt;&lt; 12) | 400, i.e. 400 pips on both buys and sells. v4 fees compound in series: 400 + 2500 × (1e6 − 400) / 1e6 = 2,899 pips ≈ 0.2899%. The same configuration was read on the NODEBOX, MYTHICAL and FRONG pools separately.

  • Each buy: the creator gets 0.25% × 40% = 10 bps;
  • Each sell: the creator gets 0;
  • With buys and sells half each, the average is 5 bps of total volume — exactly where the front end's "Receive 5bps of the 25bps LP fee on each buy" and the blog's "0.05% of the 25bps fee" come from.

Another line in the front end already has it right: the creator settings page's "Receiving 40% of the ETH side of the 25bps LP fee". Neither number is false; they're on different bases: 5bps is the average assuming half buys, half sells; 10bps is the true figure on each buy. When real trading skews to sells it falls below 5bps — FRONG's history is 62.1% sells, so the creator's actual take is about 3.8 bps.

Withdrawals from the creator vault go 100% to the beneficiary. The internal transfers of two claim transactions show the vault paying out the address's accumulated share in full in one go, with no further split:

(3) The reinvested 60% never comes back. The front end says explicitly "Protocol-held. Added to this token's liquidity — not part of your earnings and not claimable." As of 2026-08-18, the two vaults held: creator vault 6.554929 ETH unclaimed (23,475 transactions), reinvest contract 6.993135 ETH (70). About 13.55 ETH ≈ $25.7K in total — the entire fee pool the platform accumulated for creators and pools in 14 days.

2.9 The other side of "permanently locked liquidity": the maths of a collective exit

After a Crowd Launch migrates, the LP is a single-range position [−84900, +88750] whose upper price bound is about 7,147× the clearing price. This means the pool's ETH only gets swapped out when the price rises; if all buyers sell back, getting the originally injected ETH back requires the price to fall extremely far:

The supply structure at graduation is fixed: 500M tokens go to bidders; 500M go, together with all the ETH raised, into that irrevocable position (reservedTokenAmountForLP = 500,000,000; unsold supply is burned to 0xdEaD). In other words, the tokens in participants' hands exactly equal the pool's token reserve.

Under constant product, when the amount sold is s× the pool reserve, the share of funds recovered is s/(1+s), and the price falls to 1/(1+s)²:

Participants sellAmount soldFunds recovered collectivelyPrice vs clearingEquivalent drop
25%125M20.0%64.0%−36.0%
50%250M33.3%44.4%−55.6%
75%375M42.9%32.7%−67.3%
100% (dump everything)500M50.0%25.0%−75.0%

The key conclusion is the cap: even if every participant dumps every token into the pool, collectively they recover at most half their money, and the price stops at 25% of the clearing price. The other half stays in the position forever — not "hard to recover" but unrecoverable given the circulating supply. Recovering 90% would require selling 9× the pool reserve, and those tokens don't exist.

Basis: pools.trade uses a v4 single-range position rather than full-range constant product; the range [−84900, +88750] spans a price ratio of about 3.5×10⁷. Within the selling pressure participants can reach, it behaves almost exactly like constant product (the range's lower bound makes the actual recovery slightly above 50%, by 1–2 percentage points).

Grounded in SnowOn's real data: all 81.05 ETH raised went into the pool, and participants hold 500M tokens. Even if these 188 addresses all exit together, they can get back at most ~40.5 ETH; the other ~40.5 ETH stays in the position forever. On top of that, the reinvested LP fees (6.99 ETH accumulated platform-wide in 14 days) are a completely separate pot; the front end says "not claimable", and no one can take them out by any route.

This isn't a trick; it's an inherent property of constant-product AMMs, and any issuance with locked LP behaves the same. But its distributional consequence is worth spelling out: locked liquidity shifts the "exit cost" from the project to the buyers as a group. The project takes the raised ETH (in Crowd mode, the entire raise), while the ETH in the pool belongs to the LP position, held by the protocol and never withdrawable; if buyers want to leave together, they can only trample each other down this curve. This is also the structural reason SnowOn participants are broadly underwater in §4.3.

3. User journeys: three roles, as they really go

3.1 Issuers: 6 steps, free throughout, but pricing power goes to the contract

  1. Connect wallet → Launch a token. A dialog with two choices; Crowd Launch is selected by default.
  2. Fill in metadata: name, symbol, image, social links. The token is minted by TokenFactory (UERC20, 18 decimals).
  3. Choose whether to enable the creator fee. Enabling it points the LP position's recipient at the vault-backed FeeSplitter (0xeFF166AA…) and lets you set a payout address; the front end notes "Fee recipient can be updated any time".
  4. Confirm. The creator doesn't put up a cent of capital — Crowd mode has no creator self-buy; floor price, supply, duration and graduation line are all fixed by the contract and the issuer can't adjust them.
  5. The 4-hour window: the only thing to do is bring people in. The issuer has no mechanical lever during these 4 hours (can't extend, can't lower the threshold, can't top up themselves — topping up costs real money and raises everyone's cost).
  6. Settlement: if the threshold is met, the raised ETH arrives in full (fundsRecipient via the LBPStrategy migration path); if not, everything goes to zero and the token becomes a zombie.

3.2 Participants: 5 steps, with three hidden hurdles

  1. Find a live Crowd Launch. This is the first hurdle: the home page's default list is all tokens; to see live crowd sales you must click the Near graduation tab or use the All tokens ▾ → Crowd Launch filter at top right. URL parameters don't work; you have to click. Usually only 3–4 sales are live at once.
  1. Open the token page; a "How Crowd Launches work" pop-up blocks it first, and you must click Continue to use the order panel.
  2. Place an order: you can only enter an amount (with $25 / $100 / $500 shortcuts) and you see "You receive / Up to X% of supply". maxPrice is invisible (the second hurdle). The Supply calculator next to it is a pure division estimator (order ÷ graduation FDV), which the front end itself labels "Numbers provided are estimates only."
  3. Wait 4 hours. In the meantime the clearing price may have risen to where you'd never want to buy, and you have no way to actively reduce risk (you can exit the order, but filled portions are irreversible).
  4. Settlement: if it graduates, claim tokens in the portfolio; if not, claim the refund — which requires signing a transaction yourself (the third hurdle; the front-end copy says "automatically refunded").

3.3 Secondary traders: this is where the real traffic is

Almost all meaningful volume across the platform's 14 days comes from secondary trading in a handful of Instant Launch tokens, not from crowd sales. Front-end readings on 2026-08-18: FRONG FDV $4.2M / 24h volume $1.0M / 13,175 holders; swappy $1.5M / $394.9K; MYTHIC AGENT $1.4M / $147.9K. Among all graduated Crowd Launch tokens, the best, SnowOn, did only $6.5K in 24h.

4.6 Secondary-market structure: extremely concentrated

For graduated crowd tokens, the relationship between commitments and final pool liquidity is clear: pools receive liquidity of about 0.9–1.0× the amount committed (SnowOn $240.5K committed / $221.9K liquidity), because the raised ETH and the 500M reserve go together into a single-range position. But these pools barely trade.

Across the whole chain (including non-pools.trade pools), the last hour's 33,795 swaps were spread over 1,005 pools; the top 10 pools took 42.1%, the top 3 23.9%, while 463 pools (46%) had ≤2 trades in the whole hour. The head effect is more extreme than on a typical launch platform.

Now the decay of the hottest token: FRONG's day-one media snapshot was FDV $12.1M, 24h volume $20.6M, $60.96M cumulative, 12,206 buying wallets (The Defiant, 2026-08-05); by 8/18 it was FDV $4.2M, 24h $1.0M, 13,175 holders. Valuation −65%, daily volume −95%, while holder count barely moved — the classic "people still here, money no longer moving". FRONG's pool also holds only 71.71M tokens (7.2% of supply), so holdings are widely dispersed in wallets rather than in the pool.

4.7 Conversion funnel and who participates

Joining the 2,263 crowd sales with all-time BidSubmitted events (17,855 bids; 17,836 after removing one anomalous sale not priced in ETH) draws the product's real conversion funnel:

Three break points stand out:

  2. From "has a bid" to "has 5 bids" loses three-quarters (2,008 → 469). The median number of bids in sales with any bids is just 2, and 34.0% of sales have only 1. The real cause of death is not enough people.

In other words, for a project with no community, the $10K graduation threshold means gathering hundreds of retail buyers; and §4.1 shows daily launches fell 98.3% in two weeks, meaning the retail willing to show up is leaving fast. This is a funnel whose denominator is growing while its numerator shrinks.

6.2 Product risks: this is where Crowd Launch really fails

  1. All the complexity is left to users, without the matching control. Users have to understand per-block uniform pricing, the clearing-price ratchet, 13-step release and effective-amount normalization — yet the only risk knob, maxPrice, is hidden by the front end. Complexity without control is the textbook product-design flaw.
  2. No top-level entry. A product sold on "crowd sales" has no "live crowd sales" list on its home page; you need the Near graduation tab or a dropdown filter, and URL parameters don't work. Only 3–4 sales are live at once, and even those are hard to find.
  3. A 4-hour wait clashes with the user instinct of "tradeable any time". This is fatal friction for memes: memes are about immediacy and narrative windows, and CCA forces a 4-hour cooling-off period in. Launchers' 1:32 vote with their feet is the quantified result of this clash.
  4. Gaps between copy and implementation erode trust (§3.4). Especially "automatically refunded" and "no launchpad fees" — both are things users will run into themselves.

6.3 Business risks: the fee scale supports no narrative

After 14 days the two fee vaults hold 13.55 ETH ≈ $25.7K in total (creator vault 6.554929 ETH unclaimed + reinvest pool 6.993135 ETH), and chain-wide protocol fees total protocolFeesAccrued(ETH) = 0.0433 ETH. Even extrapolating linearly from the best day, this is negligible for Uniswap. So pools.trade's business goal can't be fee income; it can only be strategic positioning: grabbing the new chain's asset-issuance entry point and default AMM slot. By that standard it already has what it wanted (every graduated token's pool is a Uniswap v4 pool), at the cost of a crowd-sale product with very low usage.

6.4 Opportunities

  • CCA's right battlefield isn't memes. The whitepaper's two goals (price discovery, liquidity bootstrapping) and the disclaimer's line "CCA is available for other types of assets" already point the way: assets that need auditable, manipulation-resistant pricing — RWAs, tokenized stocks, fund units, new assets that need compliant issuance. In those settings, "4-hour window + uniform price + refund on failure" turns from a weakness into a strength. But to be clear: no such use case is visible on-chain yet. Third-party integrators have already run 722 crowd sales on the same factory; sampling shows their quote currencies are still ETH or other meme tokens (TIRED, Bark, ROBINHOODS, etc.), and most set the graduation threshold near zero. The factory is being reused, but the reusers are still launching memes.
  • There are clear, cheap product fixes: expose maxPrice (even just three presets: conservative / neutral / no limit), make "live crowd sales" a top-level entry, push refunds in a batch at migration (creators already do this by hand), and before an order show a sensitivity table — "if the clearing price rises to X you get Y" — instead of a pure division estimator.
  • The 2× structural edge of early entry is a sustainable strategy space. For active traders, the most certain return in CCA sales doesn't come from "predicting the final value" but from "sweeping floor supply with a low maxPrice in quiet sales + entering first in hot ones". Neither needs forecasting, only monitoring.
  • Stitch Crowd and Instant together: today they're completely separate. A more sensible shape is "Instant first; once a real volume threshold is reached, trigger a CCA follow-on issue", so CCA serves demand that has already been proven instead of cold-starting new coins with zero demand.

7. My four judgments

The first six chapters are mechanism and data. This chapter is my judgment as a product manager, kept separate from the facts.

7.1 Cutting fees to 0.25% cut in the wrong place

For a MEME launchpad, a 1% trading fee is the right level. Players happily accepted 3% burn taxes on early degen coins, because what MEME players care about has never been the fee but whether the coin still has volatility and upside — in a category that can do 100× in 7 days, daily doubles or halvings are normal later on, and a 0.7% fee difference is lost in the noise. Fees that are too low directly mean the platform has no income and creators have no motivation; nobody wants to drive traffic and spread the word for the coin.

Do the maths on the platform's best token (FRONG, front-end reading on 8/18: ~$1.0M daily volume):

Fee settingDaily fee poolCreator's daily incomeNotes
pools.trade actual (0.2899%)$2,899≈ $379Creators only get 10 bps on buys; FRONG's history is 37.9% buys

Over 14 days the whole platform accumulated only 13.55 ETH ≈ $25.7K in fees for creators and pools. Low fees benefited nobody: the platform gave up revenue (it never planned to take any), creators got too little to fund any promotion, and the fees buyers saved mean nothing on an asset that moves 30% intraday. This carries spot-trading values (low fees, high capital efficiency) into a market that doesn't decide on fees — the thing Uniswap is best at isn't a competitive edge here.

To be fair: low fees do cut the cost of market making and high-frequency arbitrage and genuinely help secondary liquidity; FRONG's pool depth is thick for a meme of its size. But a launchpad's bottleneck has never been secondary friction; it's whether anyone will help push the coin out. 0.25% solves a problem the latter doesn't have.

7.2 On Uniswap running its own launchpad

In the business world this is no big deal. But the timeline is worth laying out: per The Defiant (2026-08-06), Pons on the same chain upgraded to a v4 hook a week before pools.trade launched, and was listed in Uniswap's own launchpad aggregator 6 days before launch; 6 days later pools.trade launched with "zero platform fees + official brand", and its day-one token count 10,506 vs 7,210 overtook it.

Traditional internet businesses have a neutral name for this: first-party selling — the same entity provides the distribution channel and sells its own products on it. It isn't illegal and breaks no crypto-industry norm; its cost is spending part of the "neutral infrastructure" premium, which is exactly where Uniswap's credibility as an aggregation layer comes from.

The result is more noteworthy: Crowd Launch's daily launches fell 98.3% in 14 days, graduation rate 2.43%, with its sibling Instant Launch winning 32:1. One objective consequence of first-party selling is that failure also becomes publicly verifiable data.

The other side must be acknowledged too: the public goods Uniswap delivered are real — v4 pools, permanently locked LP in protocol custody, fully open-source verifiable contracts, zero issuance fees. Most launchpads' "locks" are verbal promises. On infrastructure quality, what it shipped is a notch above the rest of the sector.

7.3 The disclaimer is the most honest copy in this product

This platform may not really be meant for MEMEs. The whitepaper talks about using CCA for RWA and tokenized-stock issuance, yet pools.trade's first risk-warning pop-up says: "Any use of Pools for an asset other than a meme coin is prohibited. CCA is available for other types of assets, subject to other applicable terms and conditions." — the Pools front end Prohibited for non-meme assets, while the CCA mechanism "may be available for other types of assets, subject to other applicable terms". Translation: memes go through this front end; serious assets go through another contract.

Three pieces of evidence point the same way:

  1. The whitepaper never mentions memes. Its two goals are "price discovery" and "liquidity bootstrapping", and the authors are Hayden Adams and Ciamac Moallemi (a Columbia financial-engineering professor). That line-up doesn't write papers for meme coins.
  2. The disclaimer draws the boundary clearly, see above.
  3. The factory is already being reused externally: since launch, 722 crowd sales have used non-pools.trade configurations (custom supply, fee 3000 / 10000, custom LP recipient), going through the same CCA contracts rather than the pools.trade front end. To be accurate, though: all 722 are still meme sales (quoted in ETH or meme tokens such as TIRED, Bark, ROBINHOODS), with no RWA or tokenized-stock use case. So "CCA is reserved for serious assets" is so far only intent in the whitepaper and terms, with no on-chain evidence yet.

So this was just a stress test for pools.trade's debut — and retail paid the bill: of 2,263 crowd sales, 55 graduated; in the other 2,208, participants' money was genuinely tied up for the 4-hour window (refundable, though you have to sign a transaction to get it); and participants in the 5 graduated tokens with the largest raises are all underwater, −71% on average. In two weeks and a few thousand meme coins, Uniswap got a stress-test report on a pricing engine it can port to serious assets — and for the Web3 world as a whole, ETH bought by retail with real money took part in yet another public social experiment.

But in fairness: it said so up front. The disclaimer is the most honest piece of copy in the whole product — before your first order it tells you this isn't a mature product but an experiment explicitly confined to memes. Most launchpads don't even write that sentence.

7.4 If Crowd really took off, who would win most

ETH. Because the pools can't be withdrawn, if this model catches on, a steady stream of ETH gets locked up. And as everyone knows, most tokens eventually go to zero, except BTC and a few majors — Bitcoin stays, altcoins come and go; it's a negative-sum game from the start. Crypto really is a dark forest.

First the verifiable part: the 81.05 ETH SnowOn raised went 100% into an irrevocable single-range LP position (on-chain fundsRecipient = LBPStrategy, positionRecipient = FeeSplitter; the creator can't get the LP), with current pool liquidity of $221.9K. To swap that ETH back out, the token price has to fall, and there's a hard cap: the 500M tokens in participants' hands exactly equal the pool reserve, so dumping them all recovers at most about half (~40.5 ETH), while the price falls to 25% of the clearing price (§2.9).

But "permanently locked" is more accurate when split into two layers:

PartWhat's actually locked
ETH principal raised into the poolNot permanently destroyed, but permanently redistributed. The process of a token going to zero is precisely the process of the pool's ETH being swapped out — the ETH will always return to the market, but to whoever leaves first, not to the original funders. The pool is just a machine that moves late entrants' money to early leavers.
Reinvested 60% of LP feesThis part really is locked forever. The front end says plainly "not part of your earnings and not claimable"; 6.993135 ETH accumulated in 14 days, it only grows, and nobody has the right to take it out.
4 bp protocol feeGoes to Uniswap's TokenJar; spendable.

So the more accurate statement is: the winners are "the earliest leavers + Uniswap's protocol fee"; the only part truly burned forever is the reinvested 60%, and it happens to be the only money that "everyone paid in and no one can get back" — the purest public good in this mechanism, and the purest sunk cost.

As for "Bitcoin stays, altcoins come and go", 14 days of data don't contradict it: 2,263 crowd sales, 55 graduated (2.43%), all 5 comparable graduated tokens now below their clearing price, −71% on average; the best one is down to $6.5K of daily volume. "Negative-sum game" is accurate in the accounting sense — trading fees, protocol fees and the reinvested lock all flow out of participants' principal, with no outside cash flow coming in.

The one thing to say in its favour: compared with the old world where "the project can pull the pool and run at any time", permanent locking at least turns the path to zero into a public, continuous curve everyone can calculate in advance. It doesn't eliminate losses, but it makes them calculable — and calculability is a rare step forward in this industry.

Technical experimentCan an auction with per-block clearing, constant gas, refunds and irrevocable liquidity be built on-chainSuccess144,000 blocks of continuous clearing ran stably; the inverse-price accumulator keeps exit cost O(1); the 13-step release table sums exactly to 1e7; the model reproduces the on-chain clearing price point by point. Very high engineering quality.
Product experimentDo meme launchers and retail want to use this mechanismFailure (Crowd)Daily launches −98.3%, graduation rate 2.43% (55/2,263), median bid just $20; sibling Instant Launch won 32:1. More importantly, after graduation: all 5 comparable graduated tokens fell below their clearing price, −71% on average. Mechanism superiority didn't turn into willingness to use it, because the problems it solves (sniping, fair distribution) rank lower for meme users than "buy and sell right now".
Business experimentCan it produce meaningful revenue or a strategic assetUnprovenOnly $25.7K of fees accumulated in 14 days, so no revenue case; but the strategic position was won (default AMM + issuance entry on the new chain). Real business value depends on whether CCA can be ported to non-meme assets.

In one sentence: pools.trade is a technically beautiful experiment pointed at the wrong use case. It uses an auction mechanism meant for pricing serious assets to solve fair launches for meme coins — and the meme market doesn't really want fairness; it wants speed and narrative. Put the other way, the mechanism's real value most likely isn't in the pools.trade front end but in the CCA factory itself: in two weeks and a few thousand meme coins, Uniswap stress-tested and validated a pricing engine that can issue more serious assets. Seen that way, this wasn't a social experiment but infrastructure validation paid for with meme traffic — and it worked.

B. Definitions and what isn't covered

B1. Two easily confused fee bases. "Creator fee 5 bps" and "40% of the ETH side" are the same thing: the first is the average with half buys and half sells, the second the true figure on each buy (§2.8). Another circulating calculation treats "0.05% ÷ 0.25% = 20%" as the creator's split; that's a coincidence from dividing two different bases, not the 40/60 split executed on-chain.

B2. How graduations are counted. The front-end list doesn't show all graduated tokens, so counting from the list badly undercounts. This report's 55 graduations and 2,263 launches come from a full scan of on-chain events (§4.2, Appendix C).

B3. What this report doesn't cover. ① Whether pools.trade's trade routing charges an extra front-end interface fee was not checked. ② TokenJar's total accumulated protocol fees were not aggregated over the whole period. ③ For the 722 third-party crowd sales, 150 were sampled for quote currency and graduation status; reading requiredCurrencyRaised for every sale was not done. ④ Chain-wide volume can't be reliably derived from PoolManager Swap events (other asset pools contaminate the ETH side), so every volume figure here uses the front end's per-token readings.

C. Method and data sources

Every number in this report comes from one of four kinds of primary sources; anything quoting official wording is marked as "wording", not fact:

  • Verified contract source + constructor arguments: via the Blockscout API /api/v2/smart-contracts/{address} to fetch source_code and decoded_constructor_args; core logic read from ContinuousClearingAuction.sol, BidLib.sol, DemandLib.sol, InstantLaunchStrategy, V4FeeAdapter.
  • On-chain state and events: public RPC eth_call / eth_getLogs / eth_getCode / extsload; event signatures decoded by hand (BidSubmitted, CheckpointUpdated, BidExited, InitializerCreated, Migrated); the release table decoded 8 bytes at a time from the SSTORE2 bytecode that pointer() points to.
  • Front-end artifacts: pools.trade's JS assets fetched directly, the i18n dictionary chunk located from manifest, and 776 rhcca.* strings extracted to compare "product promises" with "contract implementation".
  • Official and media: Uniswap official blog (2026-08-05), CCA whitepaper (2025-11), The Defiant (08-05 / 08-06), pools.trade front-end readings (2026-08-18). ETH at $1,900; all dollar figures are for reference only.

Full scans added this round (reproducible): using eth_getLogs to scan all BidSubmitted events from block 27,600,000 → 39,666,441 in 250K-block slices (filtered by topic0 only, any address), giving 19,668 bids; then an address-filtered scan of LBPStrategy's InitializerCreated / Migrated, decoding words 4, 5, 7 and 8 of the migration config (reserved supply / remaining-token recipient / pool fee / tickSpacing) to separate pools.trade-style sales from third-party integrations, and finally joining bids to sales by auction address. For third-party sales, a further 150 were sampled one by one with eth_call reading currency / currencyRaised / isGraduated to judge whether their graduation thresholds are comparable.
