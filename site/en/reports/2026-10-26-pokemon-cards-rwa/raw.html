Pokémon card prices doubled offline, yet on-chain pack opening is cooling: if you hold CARDS, what should you watch?

In October the Pokémon card price index was up about 3.5× year to date, Collector Crypt's token CARDS was up 75% in 30 days, and Pump.fun and Coinbase were both adding collectible-card pack opening. The story sounds smooth.

But the data shows three things out of sync: offline hasn't ebbed, on-chain pack sales have fallen nearly 40% from June's record, the platform keeps only about $6 of profit per $100 of revenue, and holders get only about 2.2% of that. Below, the answer unfolds step by step in the order a new reader would research this sector.

In one sentence: the card sector hasn't cooled — what cooled is one way of playing it, on-chain pack opening; the opportunity isn't in "will the sector rise" but in "by what rule money flows to holders".

Background first: what kind of business are Pokémon cards

Get a few basic concepts straight first, and the data afterwards is easier to read.

Pokémon cardsA trading card game launched in Japan in 1996, turning exactly 30 in 2026. Players both collect and battle; rare cards (holos, first editions, limited runs) can sell for very high prices.
Graded cardsCards are sent to a grading company such as PSA, authenticated, scored 1–10 and sealed in a plastic slab; the higher the score the more valuable, with PSA 10 the most expensive. PSA graded over 19 million cards in 2025, nearly 10× 2020.
On-chain (RWA)Physical graded cards are stored in a vault and a matching token is issued on-chain. The token can be traded, used to open mystery packs, or redeemed for the physical card.

How big is this industry, and why has it heated up these two years

  • Nostalgia plus a new on-ramp: 2026 is the card game's 30th anniversary, and the mobile game Pokémon TCG Pocket, launched in October 2024, brought in a wave of new players.
  • Graded cards have a bottleneck: grading capacity is the gate for new high-grade cards; PSA paused its low-price tier from June and its backlog once exceeded 12 million cards. Counterfeits are rising too: Pokémon fakes +125% year on year.
  • On-chain is a very small slice: physical cards are already a large market with established buyers; on-chain platforms shorten the path to settlement and cash, they didn't create new demand.

Start from the basics: what is this sector selling?

So two lines in this sector have to be read separately: offline card prices (what the cards themselves are worth), and on-chain pack spending (how much players spend on the platforms). CARDS is the token issued by one of those platforms, Collector Crypt. We'll first see how each line moves, then where the money finally lands.

02Is the offline card market still hot?

Start at the bottom layer: how is the offline Pokémon card market itself doing?

AnswerHot. Card prices are up about 3.5× this year and doubled in the past three months; offline sales and grading volumes are rising. But historically single cards draw down deeply and recover slowly.

03Yet on-chain is cooling: did pack opening lose its appeal, or did the money run out?

Offline is hot — what about on-chain? Put card prices and on-chain pack opening side by side and a contrast appears: after June, card prices kept rising while on-chain pack opening fell back.

AnswerIt didn't lose its appeal: pack-buying wallets doubled. Big spenders spent less, and some money may have moved to trading memecoins. Demand for the cards themselves didn't weaken.

The three best-supported points so far

Big long-time players spent lessIn July, Collector Crypt's average daily spend was 39% lower in the second half than the first, while Pump.fun volume was 21% higher over the same period, possibly money moving to trading coins; this is only coincidence in time, not proof of cause.
Spending is concentrated in a few big walletsIn Q3, 548 wallets contributed 83% of spending, and packs above $2,500 were 39% of sales; a few whales can swing the total.
June was a pullback after a spikeIt was the fastest growth in half a year and coincided with Bitcoin's 21-month low; part of the fall after the peak is normal.

Sources: Blockworks, AiCoin, Crypto Briefing, Pine Analytics. "The money ran out" can't be isolated with current data.

04Did the offline heat move on-chain?

With offline this hot and on-chain cooling, the natural question is: did offline players move on-chain? First look at what they do on-chain.

AnswerNo. On-chain trading is faster and cheaper for sellers, but offline players want more trustworthy buyers and better prices, which on-chain can't offer yet. What went on-chain is mostly mystery-pack opening, not offline trading relocating.

Offline players aren't coming, mainly for four reasons

The buyers are offlineeBay, TCGplayer and card shops already have buyers, reviews and dispute handling; on-chain there aren't more buyers, just speculators who were on-chain anyway.
Collectors want the object and the experienceThey value nostalgia, the fun of opening packs and the card in hand; on-chain speed and low cost don't move them, and many collectors are wary of crypto.
Trust is differentA token points to a grader's judgment and a vault's custody, not the card itself; PSA has flagged over $200M in counterfeits, and vaults can go wrong too.
Prices are set offlineThere's no floor price on-chain; valuations rely on eBay, TCGplayer and Card Ladder data, and platform terms state that the platform decides fair value.

One exception: Courtyard has drawn some collectors who don't want to trade coins and just want better liquidity, and its physical redemptions are steady, but its scale is far smaller than pack opening, and its card sales fell from $65.2M to $25.7M between March and September.

Sources: Bankless, Decrypt, Four Pillars, Dune (Courtyard card sales).

05How does the platform make money, and how much?

If the main business on-chain is mystery packs, the platform makes money selling them. How much does it actually make?

AnswerBy selling mystery packs. Of every $100 in revenue, about $94 is cost and about $6 is profit, of which only about $0.13 went to buying CARDS.

So the platform looks more like a thin-margin mystery-box business: big volume, but most of it flows back out as sell-backs, redemptions and card purchases.

06What does holding CARDS get you?

What does the platform's income have to do with holding CARDS?

AnswerRight now only one thing: the platform sometimes spends money buying the token and burning it — just 2.2% of profit in Q3, with no dividends, no voting and no rules. Meanwhile, the tokens unlocking each month are 40–54× the buyback volume.

07What if pack opening stops working?

The platform's revenue relies almost entirely on pack opening. What if that stops working?

AnswerThe business would shrink markedly: revenue relies almost entirely on this one engine. New growth would come from new mechanics, not rising card prices.

What could bring new growth

Packs with new mechanicsMonster shows new mechanics can pull money from older platforms, but the scale is still small.
New channelsPump.fun, Coinbase and Jupiter can bring volume, but not a share for holders.
Card-backed lendingBorrowing against cards hasn't worked yet: one $53 loan secured by a $300 card was liquidated after going overdue.

Another risk is regulation: existing lawsuits focus on "whether prizes can be cashed out", and sell-backs and redemptions are exactly that path.

Sources: Blockworks, Decrypt, SEC/CFTC public filings.

08Is there an opportunity after a pullback?

Putting the facts together: after a pullback, is there still an opportunity?

AnswerConditionally. Offline hasn't ebbed, so demand for the sector is still there; but for it to become holder returns, two things must appear first: unlock pressure absorbed by the price, and a buyback rule. Only with both does the return picture improve markedly.

A checklist learned from CARDS

Does the token have a claim on profitFirst ask which document says profit goes to holders; if none, holders get nothing however good the sector is.
Is there a buyback ruleOnly a formula, a minimum and a frequency let you count it in a valuation; actions without a rule don't count.
Unlocks vs buybacksPut monthly unlocks next to monthly buybacks; if the gap is huge, be careful.
Does the platform itself make moneyLook at margins, not volume; big volume doesn't mean profit.
A token doesn't mean value capture. READY's methodology says part of revenue is used for buybacks, yet on-chain buybacks were $0 for 7 straight months; COLLECT's platform has stopped new trading. Both are second-hand and shown only for comparison.
